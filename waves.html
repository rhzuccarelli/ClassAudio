waves &
harmonics

Every sound you hear from a synthesizer is a shape in time and a set of frequencies at once. This guide lets you change the shape and watch the frequencies move, or change the frequencies and watch the shape move.

1

reading the display

Each lab in this guide has a two-part display. Both halves show the same sound.

Joseph Fourier showed that any repeating wave can be rebuilt by adding sine waves at whole-number multiples of its frequency: 1×, 2×, 3×, and so on. Those multiples are the harmonic series. The pitch you hear is set by the fundamental. The timbre, the colour of the sound, is set by how loud each harmonic is.

Press ▶ play on any lab to hear it. Only one lab plays at a time. Use the volume in the top bar, and start low if you are on speakers in a classroom.
The lin / db switch changes the spectrum scale. Linear shows loudness the way the maths writes it. Decibels (dB) squash big differences so you can see quiet high harmonics, closer to how the ear hears.
2

oscillator

An oscillator repeats one shape over and over. How many times per second it repeats is its frequency, measured in hertz (Hz), and that is the pitch. The four classic shapes come from early analog circuits. Each one has its own harmonic recipe.

2.1four shapes, four recipes

  • Sine. One bar only. No harmonics, so it sounds pure and plain, like a tuning fork or a whistle.
  • Triangle. Odd harmonics only (1, 3, 5, 7…), falling fast as 1/n². Soft and flute-like.
  • Square. Odd harmonics only, falling slowly as 1/n. Hollow and woody, like a clarinet.
  • Saw. Every harmonic (1, 2, 3, 4…), falling as 1/n. The brightest and buzziest shape, the classic start for strings and brass.
Switch between square and saw with the spectrum on db. The square has gaps where every even harmonic should be. Those gaps are why it sounds hollow.
Sharp corners and vertical jumps in the wave always mean lots of high harmonics. Smooth curves mean few.
3

additive synthesis

Additive synthesis runs Fourier forwards. You set the loudness of each harmonic yourself, and the wave is the sum of those sine waves. The 16 faders below are harmonics 1 to 16. Drag across them to draw a spectrum.

3.1building a saw, one sine at a time

Pick saw, then press build. The lab starts from a single sine and adds one harmonic every half second, each at 1/n of the fundamental. Watch the thin grey partials stack up in the wave view. The wave gets closer to a ramp with every step. The little ripple that stays near the jump is called the Gibbs effect. It only disappears with infinitely many harmonics.

3.2phase: same sound, different shape

The 0° / 180° buttons under the faders flip a harmonic upside down. The triangle preset needs every other odd harmonic flipped. Flip them back and the shape changes a lot, but the spectrum bars stay exactly the same. On a steady tone the ear barely notices, because hearing mostly follows the spectrum, not the shape.

Try the organ preset. A drawbar organ is an additive synthesizer: each drawbar is a fader for one harmonic.
4

fm synthesis

FM (frequency modulation) makes many harmonics from just two sine waves. The modulator wobbles the pitch of the carrier so fast that you no longer hear a wobble. You hear new frequencies called sidebands instead. John Chowning found this at Stanford in 1967, and Yamaha built the DX7 on it.

4.1the two controls that matter

  • Ratio (C:M) sets where the sidebands land. They appear at the carrier frequency plus and minus whole multiples of the modulator: fc ± 1·fm, fc ± 2·fm… Whole-number ratios (1, 2, 3) put them on the harmonic series and sound pitched. Ratios like 1.41 or 3.5 scatter them between harmonics. The result is inharmonic and sounds like bells, metal or noise.
  • Index sets how many sidebands are loud. At index 0 you hear the plain carrier sine. As the index goes up, energy spreads out to more and more sidebands. Their heights follow Bessel functions, so individual bars rise, fall and even drop to zero as you turn the knob.
Sidebands that would land below 0 Hz reflect back into the positive side. Where they fall on top of other sidebands, they add or cancel. That is why ratio 1:1 gives a full saw-like series and 1:2 gives odd harmonics only, like a square.
Set ratio 1.41 and turn on sweep index. A high index that falls over time is the classic FM bell or electric piano. It is bright at the strike and mellows as it decays.
5

filters

A filter does not create harmonics. It only turns existing ones up or down, depending on their frequency. This is subtractive synthesis: start with a bright, harmonic-rich wave like a saw, then carve it. The orange curve is the filter's response. Each harmonic is multiplied by the height of the curve at its frequency. The hollow grey bars and the grey wave show the sound before the filter.

5.1low pass

A low pass filter lets low frequencies through and turns high ones down. Harmonics above the cutoff fade away, and the wave loses its sharp corners. A saw with a low cutoff becomes rounder and darker. With the cutoff below harmonic 2, only the fundamental is left and the wave is almost a sine.

5.2slope and resonance

  • Slope is how steeply the filter cuts past the cutoff. A 12 db filter halves the level about every half octave. A 24 db filter cuts twice as hard. That is the classic Moog ladder sound.
  • Resonance adds a peak right at the cutoff. Any harmonic sitting on that peak gets louder than the rest. Sweep the cutoff with high resonance and you hear the harmonics called out one by one. That is the "wah" or "squelch" of acid bass lines.

5.3high pass and band pass

A high pass filter does the opposite: it removes the fundamental and low harmonics, leaving a thin, buzzy sound. Notice the pitch doesn't change even when the fundamental is almost gone. Your brain fills it in from the spacing of the harmonics. A band pass filter keeps only a narrow slice around the cutoff, like talking through a telephone or a cardboard tube.

Turn on sweep cutoff with resonance around 15 dB on a saw. Follow the orange peak through the spectrum and listen to each harmonic sing as it passes.
