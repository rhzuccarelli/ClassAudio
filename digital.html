sound design guide · part 4 · digital

digital
sound

A computer cannot store a wave. It can only store a list of numbers. This page shows how a sound becomes numbers and back again, what can go wrong on the way, what a DSP does with the numbers, and why MIDI is not sound at all.

1

the digital chain

Sound in the air is a pressure that changes smoothly. A microphone turns it into a voltage that changes in the same way. Everything up to that point is analog: it can take any value at any moment. A computer can only hold whole numbers, one after another. So every recording goes through the same chain, and every playback goes through it backwards:

2

sampling & aliasing

Sampling means measuring the voltage at regular moments and keeping only those measurements. In the left screen the grey line is the input, the orange dots are the samples, and the white line is what a DAC rebuilds from them. The right screen is the spectrum from 0 to 24 kHz. The dashed orange line marks half the sample rate.

2.1the nyquist limit

To capture a wave you need more than two samples per cycle. So the highest frequency a sample rate can store is half the sample rate, called the Nyquist frequency. Human hearing stops near 20 kHz, so a CD uses 44.1 kHz: Nyquist is 22.05 kHz, with a little room left for the filter. Video and most studios use 48 kHz.

Below Nyquist the samples hold everything. Switch the output to smooth: the white line lands exactly on the grey one, even with only three or four dots per cycle. This is the sampling theorem, and it is why digital audio does not need millions of samples per second.

2.2aliasing: frequencies fold back

Above Nyquist things go wrong. The samples of a 7 kHz sine taken at 8 kHz fit a 1 kHz sine exactly (just upside down). Once the numbers are stored, nothing can tell the two apart. So the 7 kHz tone comes out as 1 kHz. It folds back below Nyquist like a mirror: falias = |f − k·fs|. It is the same effect as wagon wheels that seem to turn backwards in a film.

Pick sine, sample rate 8k, and press sweep. The input rises all the way to 20 kHz. What you hear goes up to 4 kHz, then turns round and comes back down, again and again.

2.3the anti-alias filter

A saw wave has harmonics far above Nyquist. Sampled as it is, all of them fold back onto random places in the spectrum: the orange lines. They are not on the harmonic grid, so they sound out of tune and metallic. The only cure is to remove those frequencies before sampling, with a steep low-pass filter. After sampling it is too late. Switch the anti-alias filter on: the orange lines disappear and the sound gets duller, but it is clean.

This is also why the overdrive lab in part 3 needs oversampling. Distortion creates new harmonics after the sound is already digital, so they can fold back too.

2.4steps or smooth

A DAC holds each number until the next one arrives, which makes steps. The sharp corners of the steps are extra high frequencies, copies (images) of the spectrum mirrored around the sample rate. You can see them as hollow orange bars above Nyquist. The reconstruction filter after the DAC removes them, and the original smooth wave comes back.

The lab always plays at 48 kHz. The lower sample rates are simulated: the samples are held (steps) or interpolated with an ideal filter (smooth) before playback.
3

bit depth & dither

Each sample has to be stored as a whole number, so it is rounded to the nearest of a fixed set of levels. With N bits there are 2N levels: 16 bits give 65,536 and 24 bits give 16,777,216. The wave screen zooms in on the signal, so you always see the steps, even at low levels. The orange line underneath is the rounding error.

3.1quantization noise: 6 dB per bit

Rounding is never more than half a step off, so the error is a small, fast wobble on top of the signal: quantization noise. Every extra bit halves the step size, which lowers the noise by 6 dB. For a sine at full level the signal-to-noise ratio is about 6.02 × N + 1.76 dB: 98 dB for 16 bits and 146 dB for 24 bits. The 24-bit figure is better than any real analog circuit can manage, so the converter's own electronics set the real noise floor.

3.2quiet sounds lose bits

The step size is fixed, so a quiet sound uses fewer of the levels. At −48 dBFS a 16-bit recording only has about 8 bits left. Worse, at very low levels the error is no longer random. It repeats with the wave, so it turns into harmonic distortion: extra lines in the spectrum, and a grainy, buzzy tail on fading notes.

3.3dither

The fix sounds backwards: add a tiny bit of noise (about one step) before rounding. This is dither. The noise shakes the signal across the levels at random, so the error stops following the wave. The distortion lines in the spectrum melt into a smooth, quiet hiss, and you can even hear details smaller than one step. Every 16-bit master should be dithered as the very last step.

Choose fading note, 6 bits, listen to the error only. Without dither the error buzzes with the note and changes as it fades. Switch dither on: the error turns into plain hiss that has nothing to do with the note.
4

inside the adc

An analog-to-digital converter only needs three parts:

  • A sample-and-hold: a switch and a capacitor that freeze the voltage for a moment, so it stays still while it is measured.
  • A comparator: a circuit that answers one yes/no question: "is the input higher than this other voltage?"
  • A small DAC inside, which makes the voltages to compare against, plus some logic to choose them.

The classic design, the successive-approximation (SAR) ADC, plays "guess the number". First guess: half of full scale. Too high? Then the top bit is 0. Too low? It is 1. The next guess splits the remaining half, and so on. Every comparison gives one bit, so an 8-bit result takes 8 comparisons.

Press step one comparison at a time. On the left, the shaded band shows where the answer can still be. It halves with every step. Then switch to 12 bit: four more steps, and a step size 16 times finer.

4.1what audio interfaces really use: sigma-delta

Most audio ADCs today are sigma-delta (ΔΣ) converters. Instead of measuring precisely 48,000 times a second, they measure very roughly (1 to 5 bits) but very fast: 64 or 128 times the final rate, around 3 to 6 MHz. A feedback loop pushes the large rounding error up to ultrasonic frequencies (noise shaping). A digital filter then removes that noise and turns the fast, rough stream into 24-bit samples at 48 kHz (decimation). Bonus: because the first sampling is so fast, the analog anti-alias filter can be a simple, gentle one. The steep filtering is done in the digital filter.

5

inside the dac

A digital-to-analog converter does the opposite. Each bit of the number switches on its own share of a reference voltage, and the shares add up. The top bit (MSB) is worth half of full scale, the next bit a quarter, then an eighth, and so on down to the smallest step (LSB). Click the bits to build a number and watch the voltage stack up.

5.1the r–2r ladder

The output holds each voltage until the next sample arrives. That makes the staircase in the right screen. The reconstruction filter (chapter 2.4) then smooths it back into a wave. Switch it on to see the curve it draws through the steps.

5.2why most audio dacs are sigma-delta too

A ladder DAC is only as good as its resistors. If the MSB is 0.1 % too strong, the step from 127 to 128 is wrong every time the wave passes through the middle. That is distortion, right where quiet signals live. Most audio DACs therefore turn the samples into a very fast stream with only a few bits, and filter it. That is the sigma-delta trick from 4.1 in reverse. A few high-end "R-2R" or "multibit" DACs still use carefully trimmed ladders.

6

dsp

DSP has two meanings. Digital signal processing is any maths done on the stream of samples: every EQ, compressor, reverb, synthesizer and plugin. A DSP is a chip designed to do that maths fast. Either way, almost every audio algorithm comes down to three things: multiply samples by numbers, add the results, and remember some old samples.

The lab runs four tiny algorithms. The left screen is the impulse response: what comes out if you feed in a single click. The right screen is the frequency response: how loud each frequency comes out. The frequency response is the Fourier transform of the impulse response, the same idea as the wave and spectrum screens in part 1.

6.1a filter is just an average

The moving average outputs the mean of the last N samples. Fast wiggles cancel out in the average and slow changes survive, so it is a low-pass filter. Filters that only look at past inputs like this are called FIR (finite impulse response): their click response stops after N samples. Its frequency response has notches at multiples of 48000 / N Hz, where a whole number of cycles fits in the window and averages to zero.

6.2feedback: one multiply instead of many

The one-pole filter mixes the new input with its own previous output. Because it feeds back, the click response decays forever (in theory). This is an IIR filter (infinite impulse response). It gives a smooth low pass with only two multiplications per sample. The filters in synths and EQs are built from IIR sections like this, usually the two-pole "biquad".

6.3echo is memory

A delay just stores samples and plays them back later. 500 ms at 48 kHz is 24,000 numbers in memory. Feed some of the output back and you get repeating echoes. Make the delay very short (1–10 ms) and the echoes blur into a comb filter: the evenly spaced peaks on the right screen, the flanger from part 3.

6.4dsp chips, buffers and latency

At 48 kHz a new sample arrives every 20.8 µs, and the work has to be done before the next one. DSP chips are built around a multiply-accumulate (MAC) unit that does a = a + x × c in one clock cycle, with special memory for delay lines. You find them in audio interfaces with built-in effects, guitar pedals, phones, hearing aids and mixing desks. Plugins on a computer run "native" on the main CPU instead.

Computers process audio in blocks (buffers) rather than one sample at a time, which is much more efficient but adds delay: latency = buffer size ÷ sample rate. That is why you lower the buffer size when recording, and raise it when mixing a big session.

7

midi

MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface, 1983) carries no sound. It describes a performance: which key went down, how hard, when it came up, where the mod wheel is. The instrument that receives the messages makes the sound. MIDI is to audio what sheet music is to a recording. Change the synth, the tempo or a wrong note, and the performance plays again with the change.

Play the keyboard below with the mouse, or with the computer keys a to k (black keys on w e t y u, octave on z / x). If you have a MIDI keyboard, plug it in and press connect midi device (Chrome or Edge). Every message appears on the right, byte by byte.

7.1anatomy of a message

Most messages are three bytes. The first is the status byte. It always starts with a 1 bit. Its top four bits say what kind of message it is, and its bottom four bits say which of the 16 channels it is for. The data bytes that follow always start with a 0 bit, so they can only hold 0 to 127. That is why so much in MIDI has 128 steps.

7.2note numbers, velocity and controllers

  • Note number: 60 is middle C (C4) and 69 is A4. Each step is one semitone, so the pitch is f = 440 × 2(n − 69) / 12. Notes 0–127 cover 8 Hz to 12.5 kHz.
  • Velocity is how fast the key went down. The synth decides what it means: usually louder, and often brighter. Here it also opens the filter.
  • Control change messages move knobs and pedals: CC 1 is the mod wheel, 7 volume, 10 pan, 64 the sustain pedal, 74 brightness.
  • Pitch bend uses both data bytes together, so it has 14 bits: 16,384 steps, with 8192 in the centre. 128 steps would make audible jumps in a slow bend.

7.3cables, speed and midi 2.0

The original 5-pin DIN cable runs at 31,250 bits per second. Each byte takes 10 bits on the wire, so one three-byte note-on takes about 1 ms. A 10-note chord is therefore spread over 10 ms, which some players can feel. USB MIDI is much faster. MIDI 2.0 (2020) keeps the same ideas but adds 16-bit velocity, 32-bit controllers and two-way communication, and still works with MIDI 1.0 gear.

MIDI is also tiny. A minute of piano playing is a few kilobytes. The same minute as 48 kHz / 24-bit stereo audio is about 17 MB.

Hold a note and move the pitch bend slider. A stream of E0 messages appears, each with two data bytes. Then hold a note and raise the mod wheel: B0 01 messages, and the synth adds vibrato.
8

anatomy of a .wav file

A .wav file is the simplest way to store the numbers from chapters 2 and 3. It has a short header that says how to read the numbers, then the numbers themselves, one after another, with no compression. The lab below builds a real one-second .wav file in your browser and shows every byte of its start. Hover over a byte (or tap a row on the right) to see what it means. You can also open one of your own files: it is read on your computer and never uploaded.

8.1chunks

A wav file is a RIFF file (Resource Interchange File Format, 1991). It starts with the letters RIFF, the size of the rest of the file, and the word WAVE. After that everything is a chunk: a four-letter name, a four-byte size, and then that many bytes of content. Two chunks are required:

  • fmt (with a space at the end, so it has four letters) holds the recipe: integer or float, number of channels, sample rate, bits per sample, and two values worked out from them, the byte rate and the block align.
  • data holds the samples. Its size tells you how long the sound is: duration = data size ÷ byte rate.

Any other chunk is optional, and a program skips the ones it does not understand. That is how metadata fits in without breaking old software: LIST (title, artist, software), bext (Broadcast Wave: description, date and timecode), cue (markers), smpl (loop points for samplers), iXML (scene, take and track names from film recorders) and JUNK (empty space reserved so the header can grow later). Open a file from your DAW or a field recorder and you will often find several.

8.2little-endian: numbers stored backwards

Any number bigger than one byte is split into bytes, and a wav file stores the smallest byte first. This is called little-endian. 44,100 is 0x0000AC44 in hexadecimal, but in the file it appears as 44 AC 00 00. To read it, take the bytes right to left. Apple's AIFF format stores the same information in the other order (big-endian), which is one of the few real differences between the two.

8.3frames and interleaving

In a stereo file the channels take turns: left sample, right sample, left, right… One sample of every channel at the same moment is a frame, and the block align field says how many bytes a frame takes: 4 for 16-bit stereo, 6 for 24-bit stereo. In the lab the left channel is green and the right channel is dark green, so you can see them alternate.

How a sample is written depends on the bit depth:

  • 8 bit is unsigned: 0 to 255, with silence at 128 (80). This is an old exception, so watch out for it.
  • 16, 24 and 32 bit integers are signed (two's complement): silence is 0, and negative numbers start with FF in their top byte.
  • 32-bit float (format 3) stores the value as a decimal number, with ±1.0 as full scale. Values above 1.0 are allowed, so a float file cannot clip.
Pick 8 bit and silence: every sample byte is 80. Switch to 16 bit: now silence is 00 00. Then switch the sample rate between 44.1k and 48k and watch bytes 24–27 and 28–31 change.

8.4limits and relatives

The size fields are 32 bits, so a wav file cannot be bigger than 4 GB (some older software even stops at 2 GB). That is about 6 h 45 min of 44.1 kHz / 16-bit stereo, but only 4 h 8 min of 48 kHz / 24-bit stereo, and much less for multitrack recordings. RF64 and BW64 are wav files with 64-bit sizes for longer recordings. FLAC stores the same samples losslessly in about half the space. MP3 and AAC are lossy: they throw away what a hearing model predicts you will not notice, so the original numbers cannot be recovered.
